Chapter One

The Pattern I Could No Longer Ignore

I was a Christian before I ever became an investigator. I already believed in God, trusted Christ, and believed Scripture told the truth about the world, humanity, sin, and salvation. This book is not the story of a man arriving at faith as though God were the final conclusion of a laboratory report.

But belief and understanding are not the same thing. A man can live in a house for years before he studies its foundation. That is closer to what happened to me. As I began examining questions beyond the subjects I ordinarily associated with faith, I found connections I had not expected.

What I heard in a science classroom began to bear on what I discussed in Bible study. Questions about ethics reached into questions about the universe. Human suffering could not be kept separate from what we believed about the purpose of life.

Reality was not behaving like an accident.

That conviction did not resolve every objection or remove the need for humility. It gave me a question to investigate: if reality is created, rather than accidental, what should we expect to find?

The world is ordered enough for us to study it mathematically. Living systems contain information. We are conscious beings who reason about what we discover and experience moral obligations we did not simply choose. We also confront beauty, guilt, suffering, and hope. None of these, considered alone, establishes the whole Christian case. Together, they call for an explanation.

I wanted to know whether blind accident could account for the world we actually inhabit, or whether that world bears the marks of Mind.

The Question Beneath the Questions

I had spent enough time around thoughtful people to know that unbelief is rarely simple. Suffering and hypocrisy have driven some away from Christianity. Others have intellectual or moral objections. Some have never heard the faith presented clearly enough to distinguish its claims from the caricatures surrounding it.

I also noticed a habit of keeping questions apart. Science belonged in public discussion, while faith was considered private. Moral convictions were defended fiercely, but their foundation received less attention. Jesus might be admired as an inspiring teacher without His claims being taken seriously.

A person could marvel at the mathematical order of the universe without asking why mathematics describes it so well. Someone else could condemn injustice without considering what makes a moral obligation binding. Each subject was examined within its own boundaries, and the connections were left unexplored.

The difficulty was not necessarily a failure to recognize evidence. Often, it was a failure to consider the evidence together.

Cosmology and fine-tuning raise questions about the universe’s beginning and precision. Biology brings us to information; mathematics, to intelligibility. Consciousness and morality require an account of human personhood and obligation. Archaeology and history let us examine testimony about the past, including the life and claims of Jesus Christ.

These disciplines do not speak in the same way, and their findings cannot simply be treated as interchangeable. Yet their implications can be considered together. Does a Creator make the major features of reality more coherent than a purposeless universe does?

That became the question I could not set aside.

A Conversation That Would Not Leave Me Alone

We had been discussing evangelism: the Christian responsibility to speak truthfully and lovingly about Christ in a culture increasingly unsure that truth can be known.

I found that uncertainty troubling. People insisted on justice while rejecting the idea of an objective moral foundation. Science was celebrated, but sometimes used to rule God out before the question had been examined. Human dignity was affirmed without much explanation of why a temporary arrangement of matter should possess intrinsic worth.

These assumptions reached into ordinary life. Children encountered them in school. Families argued over them. Artificial intelligence raised questions about mind and personhood that many people had scarcely considered before. Discussions about the body shifted between treating it as meaningful and treating it as something we could disregard whenever it frustrated our desires.

People still wanted freedom, love, and purpose. I wondered whether they had considered what kind of universe could sustain those things.

Our discussion of evangelism began to change. Beyond asking how to persuade people, we were asking how to help them see reality clearly. Perhaps some had rejected Christianity without ever encountering its whole case. They knew fragments of science, objections to Scripture, or painful experiences with religious people. Those fragments could appear decisive when never considered alongside anything else.

Later, in a quieter conversation with my wife, Kathryn, I tried to explain what was taking shape. I had no chapter order or finished method. I said something close to this:

“I don’t think the problem is lack of evidence. I think the evidence has been separated into locked rooms. What happens if we open the doors?”

Kathryn did not answer immediately. She had learned by then that when I said something like that, I was usually not asking for an answer so much as listening to the thought myself.

Finally she said, “Then maybe you should open them.”

I carried that sentence with me. It gave direction to the investigation I was beginning.

Veritology

I needed a name for an inquiry that could draw on science, theology, philosophy, history, and personal testimony without pretending any one of them exhausted the subject.

The word I settled on was Veritology.

I tried the word aloud once before I trusted it on the page. Kathryn looked at me as though she was deciding whether I had named a method or invented a disease.

“Veritology?”

“Truth. Investigation.”

She considered it for another second.

“I’ve heard worse.”

That was enough encouragement to keep it.

By Veritology, I mean the disciplined investigation of reality, following truth wherever it leads.

For that to mean anything, I had to define terms carefully and examine explanations honestly, including the ones I preferred. Evidence could not be excluded merely because its implications were uncomfortable. Nor could I decide in advance that a difficult part of human experience was irrelevant to the inquiry.

A worldview must account for the physical universe and for the people who live in it. Describing a person’s chemistry does not exhaust the questions raised by consciousness, moral responsibility, or grief. Likewise, treating Jesus as a harmless moral teacher leaves His claims unexamined.

The investigation had to make room for the genetic code and the moral law, for the structure of the cosmos and the testimony of first-century witnesses. Whatever explanation I accepted would have to address the world in its fullness.

The First Chain

My first line of reasoning began with order. Order makes intelligibility possible, and intelligibility reveals rational structure. Within that structure, information can be recognized, transmitted, and understood. Information raises questions about mind, and mind raises questions about its source. Following that chain brings us to the question of a Creator.

This was a direction for inquiry, not a complete argument compressed into a few sentences.

We live among regularities dependable enough to investigate. We can calculate the effects of gravity, study light, and understand chemical processes that sustain life. Science is possible because the world has structure and our minds can grasp at least some of it.

Why should those two things correspond? Why should a universe produced by blind, unguided processes produce minds capable of discovering its laws? Mathematics is developed in the mind, yet describes the external world with extraordinary power.

Other questions extend beyond scientific description. Moral obligation presses upon us as though right and wrong are more than preferences. Beauty moves us. Suffering strikes us as something wrong with the world, rather than simply something happening within it.

These questions concern the conditions under which science, reason, and human meaning are possible. The Christian claim reaches beyond placing God in an unexplained gap. God is the reason there is a knowable world at all.

Not an Argument Against Science

I regard science as one of the great witnesses against a purposeless universe. Its success gives us reason to ask why reality is open to rational investigation.

The choice between God and science is therefore a false one. Science plainly works. What requires examination is the claim that it works in a universe without intention, purpose, or rational foundation.

Science describes laws and regularities. Their existence still calls for explanation. Studying biological information does not make questions about its origin philosophically irrelevant. Observing brain activity does not, by itself, explain conscious experience or establish why our reasoning should be trustworthy.

The limits are especially clear in morality. We can measure human behavior, but measurement alone cannot establish our obligation to do good and avoid evil. A microscope cannot tell us whether murder is wrong. Nor can a telescope, however much it reveals, tell us why anything exists.

Recognizing those limits allows science to do its work without demanding that it answer every kind of question. Historical inquiry also depends on more than an object under examination: an artifact must be understood through context, reason, and judgment.

The difficulty arises when one valid description is treated as a complete explanation. That is reductionism. It reduces love to instinct and consciousness to computation, then assumes nothing remains to be explained. Applied to Christianity, it can turn faith into psychology, sin into dysfunction, and salvation into self-improvement. Jesus becomes merely a teacher.

Such explanations achieve simplicity by leaving out what they were supposed to explain. Veritology requires us to examine what has been omitted.

The Messy Middle

An investigation involving human beings will encounter inconsistency. Christians can speak truth without love or fail to live by what they profess. Skeptics can raise fair objections. Intelligence does not prevent pride, and sincerity does not guarantee that a person is right.

Evangelism takes place amid those difficulties. It happens in divided families and in conversations after a funeral. Online, a slogan may reach someone long before a careful explanation does. At a job site or kitchen table, a question about God may arise from pain that the speaker has never described.

I am naturally drawn to the case—to the question, the evidence, the answer. I have had to remind myself that a sound argument does not relieve me of the responsibility to listen to the person across from me.

People are not arguments with pulse rates. They are image-bearers. Their questions come with memories, wounds, and loyalties I may know nothing about. Christian witness must address the mind while caring for the person.

Christianity makes a claim about reality, including creation, human sin, and redemption through Jesus Christ. It cannot be reduced to a private source of comfort or a tradition we place around the edges of life. Its claims should withstand examination, but evidence must never become an excuse to treat someone cruelly.

Truth is not less true when spoken gently. And gentleness is not less gentle when it refuses to lie.

Evangelism

Learning to speak clearly is useful. Reducing evangelism to a technique is another matter. A person is not a sale to close, and agreement won through pressure is not the purpose of Christian witness.

Sometimes the conversation must begin earlier than we expect. Someone who understands morality as preference may struggle to understand sin. Someone who regards human life as an accident may need to reconsider that belief before understanding salvation. A discussion of Scripture can stall if truth itself is treated as personal choice.

Those questions deserve answers. Recognizing creation, meaning, and accountability can help a person understand what Christians mean when we speak of Christ as Redeemer.

This does not require a lecture on cosmology or DNA in every conversation. It requires enough understanding to respond wisely to the question actually being asked. Sometimes that question is intellectual; sometimes it comes through anger or grief. We need to listen carefully enough to know the difference.

Veritology begins with the conviction that truth exists, reason can be used to pursue it, and evidence can be considered together. Reality is more coherent under God than without Him. That is not the whole Gospel, but it may clear the ground so the Gospel can be heard.

The Pattern

As the investigation developed, I began to see connections among arguments I had once considered separately. The evidence formed a more coherent account of the world, and its implications led me toward Christ.

The inquiry also made demands on me. If I asked others to follow truth wherever it led, I would have to be willing to do the same.

I do not ask readers to dismiss suffering, doubt, abuse, hypocrisy, or the failures of religious people. Those are real, and some must be faced directly in the chapters ahead. I ask that they be considered without closing off the rest of the inquiry.

The doors Kathryn encouraged me to open could not remain closed to subjects I found difficult. Science, morality, history, and the claims of Jesus all belonged in the investigation. I wanted to know which worldview could account for them together.

That is the work I came to call Veritology. For me, it began with a realization I could no longer ignore:

Reality was not behaving like an accident.
