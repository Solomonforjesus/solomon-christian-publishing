Chapter Three

The Universe Can Be Understood

Steve had pointed me toward the beginning.

Veronica pressed the next question.

It was one thing to say the universe had a beginning. It was another thing to notice that the universe could be understood. That difference became important to me.

A beginning raises the question of cause.

Intelligibility raises the question of mind.

Veronica saw that clearly. She was not dramatic about it. She did not treat mathematics as a magic trick or a secret code reserved for the gifted. She spoke of it with restraint, as though the subject deserved respect without needing decoration.

That was one of the reasons I listened to her. She also had a habit of straightening whatever was in front of her before making a difficult point—a notebook, a stack of paper, once even a coffee cup that was already straight enough.

I noticed it because the gesture usually meant I was about to hear something carefully considered.

She had the rare ability to make mathematics feel both ordinary and astonishing. Ordinary, because numbers are part of daily life. We count money, measure lumber, balance checkbooks, follow recipes, mark time, compare distances, and calculate risk. Astonishing, because beneath those ordinary uses is a deeper fact most people rarely stop to consider.

Mathematics works.

Not sometimes.

Not locally.

Not merely as a human convenience.

It works with a consistency that reaches from a kitchen table to the motion of planets, from a child’s counting blocks to the equations of physics, from a carpenter’s square to the structure of galaxies.

That is not a small thing.

It is one of the quiet miracles behind science itself.

What Mathematics Is

Before making too much of mathematics, we should define what we mean.

Mathematics is the study of number, quantity, structure, relation, pattern, and form.

That sounds simple, but the implications are enormous. Mathematics does not merely help us count objects. It describes relationships built into reality. It allows us to measure, compare, predict, and reason.

Bridges stand, satellites reach orbit, and medicine calculates dosage because mathematics is reliable.

Engineering, physics, architecture, chemistry, finance, astronomy, aviation, and computer science all depend on the same expectation:

Reality has structure, and that structure can be described.

If that expectation failed, science would fail with it.

This is why mathematics belongs near the beginning of the investigation. It is not merely another academic subject. It is one of the conditions that makes the scientific search possible.

Science depends on measurement, measurement on mathematics, and mathematics on intelligible structure.

Why is reality mathematical at all?

Discovered, Not Invented

Veronica once explained the point with a simplicity I never forgot.

“People say we invented mathematics,” she said. “But that is only partly true. We invented symbols. We invented notation. We invented ways to write mathematics down. But we did not invent the truths those symbols describe.”

I pushed back.

“So we discover mathematics?”

“Some of it,” she said. “At least the truths the symbols point toward.”

“That sounds more philosophical than mathematical.”

She gave me the smallest smile.

“Mathematics becomes philosophical very quickly when you ask what it is.”

That answer stayed with me because it was exactly the kind of answer Veronica gave. She would not pretend a question was smaller than it was merely to make it easier.

That distinction matters.

Human beings invented the written symbol “2.” We invented the plus sign. We invented the equal sign. We developed languages, systems, diagrams, proofs, and textbooks.

But we did not invent the fact that two objects plus two objects make four objects.

We did not invent the relationship between the sides of a right triangle.

We did not invent the ratios that govern circles.

We did not invent prime numbers.

We discovered them.

Long before any human being wrote an equation, the relationships mathematics describes were already present. Before Pythagoras, before Euclid, before Newton, before Einstein, before calculators, chalk, or whiteboards, reality already possessed structure.

The triangle did not wait for man to understand it.

The circle did not wait for man to name it.

The stars did not wait for man to calculate their motion.

Mathematics is not powerful because human beings imposed it on a meaningless universe. Mathematics is powerful because the universe was already structured in a way the mind could gradually uncover.

That is a very different claim.

It means knowledge is not creation from nothing. It is recognition.

We are not inventing reality.

We are learning to read it.

A Simple Theorem

The Pythagorean theorem is worth mentioning for that reason.

Not because it is complicated.

Because it is simple, ancient, and still true.

In a right triangle, the relationship between the sides is expressed this way:

a² + b² = c²

The squares of the two shorter sides, added together, equal the square of the longest side.

That is the basic idea.

If one side is 3 and another is 4, the longest side is 5.

3² + 4² = 5²
9 + 16 = 25

This is not impressive because the arithmetic is difficult. It is impressive because the relationship is real. It was real before anyone named it. It remains true whether a student understands it or not. It works in carpentry, surveying, architecture, navigation, engineering, and physics because it describes something stable about spatial reality.

That is why it belongs in this chapter.

The point is not to give a geometry lesson.

The point is to show that reality contains discoverable order.

Mathematics gives us access to that order.

A man can build a roof, measure a field, design a bridge, or send a spacecraft across millions of miles because the universe does not behave like nonsense. It has structure. It keeps its promises. It allows the mind to find patterns that were already there.

That should make us pause.

Why should that be true?

Why should a physical universe be describable by abstract mathematics?

Why should symbols written by a human hand correspond to realities far beyond human scale?

Why should the same mathematical principles function across the earth and in the heavens?

These questions are not gaps in mathematics. They are questions about why mathematics fits the world so well.

Einstein’s Question

Albert Einstein saw the mystery clearly.

He famously observed, “The most incomprehensible thing about the universe is that it is comprehensible.”

That sentence carries more weight than it first appears to carry.

Einstein was not saying the universe is easy to understand. It is not. He was saying that the universe is understandable at all. That is the surprise.

Human beings are small creatures living on a small planet. Our lives are brief. Our senses are limited. Our minds are finite. Yet we can discover laws governing gravity, light, motion, energy, and time. We can write equations that describe events no eye has seen. We can predict eclipses, calculate orbits, split atoms, transmit signals, and peer into the early universe.

That should not be treated casually.

If the human mind were merely the accidental byproduct of blind material processes, aimed only at survival, why should it also be capable of discovering deep mathematical truths about the cosmos?

Survival might explain why we notice food, danger, shelter, and reproduction.

It does not easily explain why the mind can understand black holes, quantum behavior, geometric proofs, or the expansion of the universe.

This does not mean every mathematical discovery proves God in one step. That would be too quick.

It means mathematics adds another layer to the larger case.

The universe is not only there.

It is not only ordered.

It is intelligible.

And human reason is fitted, at least in part, to understand it.

The Reason Science Works

This is one reason science has always had a deeper foundation than many people admit.

Science assumes that the universe is rationally ordered. It assumes that experiments can be repeated. It assumes that causes and effects can be studied. It assumes that numbers mean the same thing today that they meant yesterday. It assumes that the human mind can follow evidence toward truth.

Those assumptions are not scientific conclusions.

They are the foundation science stands on.

A scientist does not prove the reliability of reason by using reason. He must already trust reason in order to conduct the proof. He does not prove that nature is orderly before doing science. He must assume enough order to begin.

That is not a weakness.

It is simply a reminder that science rests on deeper realities.

The Christian worldview expects those realities.

If God is rational, and if creation reflects His order, then a rationally structured universe is not surprising. If human beings are made in the image of God, then human reason, though limited and fallen, is not an accident. We should expect some meaningful correspondence between the mind and the world.

That is exactly what we find.

The universe is not fully transparent to us. Much remains mysterious. But mystery is not the same as chaos.

Mathematics often reveals order before we understand its full meaning.

That is a remarkable feature of reality.

The Limits of the System

That may sound strange. Mathematics is extraordinarily powerful, but its formal systems are not unlimited in what they can establish. Mathematics can measure distance, but not worth. It can calculate force, but not moral obligation. It can describe patterns with astonishing precision, but it cannot tell us why a pattern-bearing universe exists in the first place.

This became important when Veronica spoke about Kurt Gödel.

Gödel’s work is difficult, and this is not the place for a technical treatment. But one central implication is useful for our investigation: sufficiently powerful, consistent formal systems contain true mathematical statements that cannot be proved from within those systems themselves.

In plain terms, formal proof has limits. A system may be unable to establish every truth expressible within its own mathematical language.

That does not prove Christianity. Nor does Gödel’s theorem prove that the physical universe requires God. To claim either would misuse the mathematics.

But Gödel does challenge the old dream that one sufficiently powerful formal system could close over every mathematical truth and prove everything from within itself. At minimum, it gives us a disciplined warning against treating any formal description as though it were automatically complete and self-sufficient.

There are boundaries.

There are truths a given system cannot prove from within its own rules.

There are limits to formal completeness.

That limitation harmonized with a caution already present in the investigation.

Cosmology had raised the question of whether the universe could account for its own existence. Gödel raised a different question within mathematics: whether a sufficiently powerful formal system could prove every truth available within its own language.

The arguments were not identical, and one did not prove the other.

But together they warned me against confusing a successful description of reality with an ultimate explanation of reality.

Reality was intelligible.

That did not mean it was self-explanatory.

More Than Utility

One of the errors of modern thinking is to treat mathematics as merely useful.

It is useful, of course. That is obvious. But usefulness is not the whole story.

A key can be useful because it fits a lock. The usefulness points to a relationship between the key and the lock. Mathematics is useful because it fits reality. The fit is the mystery.

Why should abstract thought fit the physical world?

Why should equations written on a whiteboard describe the motion of planets?

Why should geometry imagined in the mind correspond to space outside the mind?

Why should mathematical reasoning allow us to discover things no person has directly experienced?

The more I considered those questions, the more difficult it became to treat mathematics as a human invention laid over an accidental universe.

Mathematics felt less like a tool we created and more like a language we learned.

Not language in the ordinary sense of spoken words and grammar. Dawn would later help me think more carefully about that. But mathematics did seem to function as a kind of rational grammar beneath the physical world.

It did not speak with a voice.

But it could be read.

Veronica’s Contribution

Veronica did not push me toward a shallow conclusion.

She did not say, “Mathematics exists, therefore Christianity is true.”

That would have been too careless.

Her contribution was more precise. By then I had begun to recognize that precision as part of her character. Veronica rarely gave me the strongest sentence available. She gave me the strongest sentence she believed the evidence could carry.

That restraint mattered. It was becoming part of the method I was trying to build.

She helped me see that mathematics belonged within the larger pattern. Its testimony was distinct, but it did not stand alone.

The universe began.

The universe is ordered.

The universe permits life.

And now, the universe can be understood.

That sequence matters.

If reality were accidental at the deepest level, intelligibility would be a surprise. If the human mind were only a survival machine, its ability to grasp abstract truths would be difficult to explain. If mathematics were merely invented, its power over the physical world would be strange.

But if reality is created by Mind, then mathematics begins to make sense.

Not completely.

But coherently.

That word mattered more and more to me.

Coherence.

Christianity did not merely give me doctrines to believe. It gave me a framework in which the features of reality began to belong together.

None of this removed mystery. It deepened it. But the mystery was no longer empty. It was ordered. It had direction. It seemed to invite pursuit.

The Next Door

By the time I began to understand Veronica’s point, the investigation had moved again.

Cosmology had asked why the universe exists.

Mathematics asked why the universe can be understood.

But another question was waiting.

If mathematics reveals structure, language carries meaning.

Human beings do not merely calculate. We speak. We name. We interpret. We communicate. We use symbols to carry thought from one mind to another.

That fact would matter more than I first realized.

Because the next stage of the investigation would not begin with numbers.

It would begin with words.
