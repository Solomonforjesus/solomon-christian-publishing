Chapter One

The Pattern I Could No Longer Ignore

I was a Christian before I ever became an investigator.

That matters.

This book is not the story of a man reasoning his way into belief as though God were a late conclusion at the end of a laboratory report. I already believed in God. I already trusted Christ. I already believed Scripture told the truth about the world, humanity, sin, and salvation.

But belief and understanding are not the same thing.

A man can stand inside a house for years before he begins to study its foundation. He can trust the roof over his head without knowing the structure beneath it. He can live safely within its walls and later discover that the architecture is stronger, deeper, and more intentional than he first understood.

That is closer to what happened to me.

I did not begin with unbelief. I began with confidence. Over time, however, that confidence began gaining structure. The truths I had received by faith began meeting evidence across disciplines and questions I had not expected to connect.

At first, these seemed like separate subjects.

One belonged in a science classroom.

Another in a Bible study.

Another in a conversation about ethics.

Another in a documentary about the universe.

Another in the quiet ache of human suffering.

But the more closely I looked, the less separate they appeared.

A pattern began to form.

Reality was not behaving like an accident.

That statement may sound simple, but it is not small. It does not settle every question, answer every objection, remove mystery or grief, or eliminate the need for humility. But it does expose the central issue.

If reality is accidental, we should expect one kind of world.

If reality is created, we should expect another.

And the world we inhabit contains features that demand careful attention.

It is ordered.

It is intelligible.

It contains information.

It supports consciousness.

It confronts us with moral obligation.

It permits mathematical description.

It preserves history.

It confronts us with beauty, guilt, longing, death, and hope.

No single one of these realities establishes the entire case. That is not how serious reasoning works. Taken together, however, they create pressure. They form a cumulative case. They ask whether blind accident offers the best explanation for reality—or whether reality bears the marks of Mind.

This is where the investigation began to change for me.

Not because I stopped believing.

Because I began noticing.

The Question Beneath the Questions

The first real tension was not scientific. It was human.

I had spent enough time around thoughtful people to know that unbelief is rarely simple. Some people reject Christianity because they have suffered. Some because they have been wounded by hypocrisy. Some because they were taught that faith is intellectually unserious. Some because they have moral objections. Some because they have never heard Christianity presented with clarity, humility, and evidence.

And some reject it because they have learned to keep truth in separate rooms.

Science goes in one room.
Morality goes in another.
Faith is placed somewhere private.
History is handled selectively.
Personal meaning is treated as emotional.
Jesus is allowed to be inspiring, perhaps, but not authoritative.

That arrangement works for a while.

It lets a person admire the mathematical order of the universe without asking why mathematics describes physical reality so effectively. It allows someone to marvel at DNA without lingering too long over the information it carries. It permits moral outrage against injustice while avoiding the question of where objective moral obligation comes from. It allows consciousness to be treated as an accident, even while every argument against God depends on the trustworthiness of conscious reasoning.

It is not that intelligent people fail to see the evidence.

Many see it clearly.

The problem is that the evidence is often separated before it is allowed to accumulate.

That word matters: accumulate.

One isolated fact may be dismissed. Two may be debated. A handful may be explained away. But when independent lines of evidence begin leaning in the same direction, a fair mind should pause.

Cosmology raises questions about beginnings.

Fine-tuning raises questions about precision.

Biology raises questions about information.

Mathematics raises questions about intelligibility.

Consciousness raises questions about personhood.

Morality raises questions about authority.

Archaeology raises questions about memory, place, and testimony.

History raises questions about Jesus Christ.

The question is not whether every discipline says the same thing in the same way. They do not. The question is whether the major features of reality are more coherent under a Creator framework than under a purposeless one.

That became the question I could not set aside.

A Conversation That Would Not Leave Me Alone

The moment did not arrive with thunder.

It came in an ordinary setting, the kind of setting where important thoughts sometimes enter quietly and then refuse to leave.

We had been discussing evangelism. Not marketing. Not church growth technique. Evangelism. The plain Christian responsibility to speak truthfully and lovingly about Christ in a culture increasingly unsure that truth exists at all.

That is one of the defining tensions of our time.

We live in a culture that talks constantly about truth while doubting that truth can be known. People insist on justice while often rejecting the moral foundation that makes justice more than preference. We celebrate science but sometimes treat science less as a disciplined search for truth and more as a boundary marker against God. We speak of human dignity yet struggle to explain why human beings possess intrinsic worth if they are only temporary arrangements of matter.

This is not merely an academic problem. It shapes homes, classrooms, politics, medicine, churches, and the way children are taught to understand themselves.

The confusion had become difficult to miss. Technology was advancing faster than wisdom. Artificial intelligence was raising new questions about mind, language, creativity, and personhood. Biology was being discussed as though the body were both sacred and disposable, fixed and fluid, meaningful and meaningless, depending on the argument of the moment. Public morality had become intense, but often rootless. People still wanted justice, identity, love, freedom, and purpose. They simply wanted those goods without always asking what kind of universe can sustain them.

That was the cultural air.

So the evangelism question became more than, “How do we persuade people?”

It became, “How do we help people see reality clearly again?”

Somewhere in that discussion, an idea began to take shape.

Maybe many people were not rejecting Christianity because they had examined the whole case and found it wanting.

Maybe they had never been shown the whole case.

Maybe they had inherited fragments.

A little science here.
A little skepticism there.
A little moral outrage.
A little personal pain.
A little caricature of Christianity.
A little suspicion of Scripture.
A little admiration for Jesus, carefully separated from His claims.

Fragments can feel persuasive when they are never brought together.

But truth does not fear being brought together.

That thought stayed with me.

Later, in a quieter conversation with my wife, Kathryn, I found myself trying to say it plainly. I did not have the whole structure yet. I did not have the chapter order. I did not have the language polished. But the burden was becoming clear.

I said something close to this:

“I don’t think the problem is lack of evidence. I think the evidence has been separated into locked rooms. What happens if we open the doors?”

Kathryn did not answer immediately. She had learned by then that when I said something like that, I was usually not asking for an answer so much as listening to the thought myself.

Finally she said, “Then maybe you should open them.”

It was a simple sentence. She probably did not know how long I would carry it.

That was the beginning of the method.

Not the beginning of faith.

The beginning of the investigation.

Veritology

I needed a word for the kind of investigation I had in mind.

Not science, theology, philosophy, history, or personal testimony alone.

Each of those matters. Each has its proper place. But the question I was asking required a disciplined willingness to let all truth speak.

The word I settled on was Veritology.

I tried the word aloud once before I trusted it on the page. Kathryn looked at me as though she was deciding whether I had named a method or invented a disease.

“Veritology?”

“Truth. Investigation.”

She considered it for another second.

“I’ve heard worse.”

That was enough encouragement to keep it.

By Veritology, I mean the disciplined investigation of reality, following truth wherever it leads.

It is not a trick word, nor is it an attempt to dress faith in borrowed intellectual sophistication. It is simply a way of naming the method.

Follow truth.

Do not force the conclusion too early.

Do not protect a preferred worldview from examination.

Do not confuse mystery with contradiction.

Do not demand that one discipline answer questions outside its reach.

And do not isolate evidence simply because the cumulative weight becomes uncomfortable.

Veritology asks several basic questions:

What do we actually know?

What is the best explanation of what we know?

What assumptions are we bringing to the evidence?

Do those assumptions clarify reality—or shield us from it?

What happens when independent disciplines converge?

And finally:

Which worldview explains the whole with the fewest contradictions and the greatest coherence?

That last question would become increasingly important.

Every worldview explains some things well.

The real test is whether it can carry the whole weight.

What Kind of Evidence Counts?

One of the first mistakes in these conversations is to treat evidence as though it comes in only one form.

It does not.

A fingerprint is evidence.

A mathematical relationship is evidence.

A historical inscription is evidence.

Eyewitness testimony is evidence.

Genetic information is evidence.

Moral experience is evidence.

Conscious awareness is evidence.

The existence of a universe with stable laws is evidence.

Evidence must be weighed according to the kind of question being asked.

If we are investigating whether a particular person was present at a crime scene, DNA may matter enormously.

If we are asking whether Caesar crossed the Rubicon, DNA is probably irrelevant.

If we are asking whether two plus two equals four, archaeology will not settle it.

If we are asking whether murder is wrong, a telescope cannot answer.

The modern temptation is to treat empirical science as though it were the only path to knowledge. But that claim defeats itself because the statement “empirical science is the only path to knowledge” is not itself a conclusion of empirical science. It is philosophy.

That does not weaken science.

It places science where it belongs: as one extraordinarily powerful way of knowing within a larger rational framework.

History has methods.

Mathematics has methods.

Logic has methods.

Philosophy has methods.

Science has methods.

Personal testimony has evidentiary value, though it must be weighed carefully.

Even ordinary life depends on forms of knowledge we cannot place under a microscope.

I know my wife loves me.

I cannot prove that with a particle accelerator.

I know cruelty is wrong.

I cannot place wrongness on a scale.

I know the past existed.

I cannot rerun yesterday in a laboratory.

I know my thoughts are real.

I cannot hand one to another person for measurement.

Yet none of these beliefs is irrational.

The question is not whether something can be weighed.

The question is whether we have good reason to believe it is true.

That distinction would allow the investigation to move freely across disciplines without pretending they were identical.

From Evidence to Explanation

Evidence by itself does not announce its interpretation.

It must be explained.

This is where worldviews enter the room.

Suppose two people look at the same biological system.

Both see complexity.

Both see information.

Both see function.

Both may agree on the chemistry.

Yet one interprets the system through a framework in which mind, purpose, and design are excluded before the investigation begins. The other permits design as a possible explanation if the evidence warrants it.

They may agree on the data and still disagree about the conclusion.

That does not mean evidence is meaningless.

It means assumptions matter.

Every investigator has them.

The honest question is whether our assumptions are serving the evidence or controlling it.

This became one of the central disciplines of Veritology.

Try to identify the assumption before accepting the conclusion.

If someone says, “Science has shown there is no need for God,” ask whether science actually showed that—or whether philosophical naturalism supplied the conclusion.

If someone says, “Morality is just preference,” ask whether that statement matches how people actually respond to torture, genocide, abuse, betrayal, and injustice.

If someone says, “Consciousness is only brain chemistry,” ask whether describing neural activity is the same as explaining subjective awareness.

If someone says, “The universe simply exists,” ask whether that is an explanation or the refusal to seek one.

The goal is not to play word games.

The goal is to separate evidence from assumptions so both can be examined.

The First Pattern: Order

The earliest pattern I noticed was order.

Not merely neatness.

Order in the deeper sense.

Reality behaves according to regularities that can be discovered, described, tested, and used.

The universe is not merely there.

It is intelligible.

That difference is enormous.

An intelligible universe invites investigation because it behaves as though reason can reach it.

That fact sits so close to us that we often overlook it.

Every scientific experiment assumes nature will behave consistently enough for the result to mean something.

Every equation assumes relationships can be expressed rationally.

Every measurement assumes the world is stable enough to measure.

Every inference assumes the human mind can connect cause and effect.

Science depends on order before science explains anything.

But where does that order come from?

One can answer, “That is simply how the universe is.”

But that is not much of an explanation.

It is a stopping point.

The question remains:

Why is reality intelligible?

Why does mathematics work?

Why are there stable laws?

Why are human minds capable of discovering them?

Why does information exist in biology?

Why do conscious persons inhabit a universe that can be understood?

The Christian worldview has an answer.

Reality is intelligible because it is the product of Mind.

Order reflects order.

Reason reflects Reason.

Information raises questions about mind.

Mind raises questions about source.

And source brings us to the question of a Creator.

That chain does not answer every question in one move. It is not meant to. But it gives the investigation a direction.

Consider order.

We do not live in a chaotic universe where events happen without reliable structure. We live in a universe governed by regularities. Gravity behaves consistently enough to calculate. Light behaves consistently enough to study. Chemistry behaves consistently enough to sustain life. Biological systems operate through astonishingly precise arrangements. The human mind can investigate these things because reality is not nonsense.

That should make us pause.

Science depends on an intelligible world—one ordered enough to study and accessible, at least in part, to the human mind.

But why should that be true?

Why should a universe produced by blind, unguided processes also produce minds capable of discovering its laws?

Why should mathematics, developed in the mind, describe the external world with such power?

Why should information sit at the heart of living systems?

Why should moral obligation press upon human beings as though some things are truly right and others truly wrong?

Why should beauty move us?

Why should suffering feel like an invasion rather than merely an event?

These are not small questions.

They are not gaps in science. They are questions about the conditions that make science, morality, reason, and meaning possible in the first place.

The Christian claim is not that God hides in what we do not know.

The Christian claim is deeper.

God is the reason there is a knowable world at all.

Not an Argument Against Science

This must be said early and clearly.

This book is not an argument against science.

It is an argument that science, properly understood, is one of the great witnesses against a purposeless universe.

That distinction matters.

Too many people have been trained to think the choice is between God and science. That is a false choice. The real question is not whether science works. It plainly does. The question is why science works in a universe supposedly without intention, purpose, or rational foundation.

Science reveals order.

It does not explain why order exists.

Science describes laws.

It does not explain why there are laws rather than chaos.

Science studies information in living systems.

It does not make the origin of biological information philosophically irrelevant.

Science can observe brain activity.

It does not thereby explain why conscious experience exists or why reason should be trusted.

Science can describe human behavior.

It cannot, by measurement alone, establish why human beings are morally obligated to do good and avoid evil.

Those are not attacks on science. They are boundaries. Every discipline has them.

A microscope is powerful, but it cannot tell you whether murder is wrong.

A telescope can show galaxies, but it cannot tell you why there is something rather than nothing.

A particle accelerator can reveal astonishing features of matter, but it cannot tell you whether your life has intrinsic meaning.

A historical artifact can confirm a place, a people, or a practice, but interpretation still requires reason, context, and judgment.

The problem is not science.

The problem is reductionism.

Reductionism is the habit of treating part of reality as though it explains the whole. It takes one valid level of description and pretends every other level is unnecessary.

A human being becomes chemistry.

Love becomes instinct.

Morality becomes preference.

Consciousness becomes computation.

Faith becomes psychology.

Jesus becomes a teacher.

Sin becomes dysfunction.

Salvation becomes self-improvement.

Reductionism always sounds efficient. It promises simplicity. But it often achieves simplicity by leaving out the most important parts of the thing being explained.

Veritology refuses that shortcut.

The Messy Middle

A serious investigation must be honest about the mess.

The world is not tidy. Human beings are not tidy. Churches are not tidy. Science is not always practiced with perfect humility. Religious people do not always represent God well. Brilliant people can be foolish in one area and insightful in another. Sincere people can be sincerely wrong. Christians can speak truth without love. Skeptics can raise fair questions. Pain can distort judgment. Pride can do the same.

Evangelism does not happen in a clean room.

It happens in families divided by belief. It happens online, where slogans travel faster than thought. It happens in schools where children absorb assumptions long before they learn how to examine them. It happens in hospitals, funeral homes, job sites, courtrooms, church foyers, kitchen conversations, and private moments when a person wonders whether God is real and whether anyone sees their suffering.

That is why this book cannot merely be a collection of arguments.

Evidence, definitions, and logic matter. But people are not arguments with pulse rates.

I had to remind myself of that because I am naturally drawn to the case—to the question, the evidence, the answer. It is possible to become so interested in whether an argument is sound that you forget there is a human being sitting across from you.

They are image-bearers. They carry memories, wounds, desires, loyalties, fears, sins, hopes, and questions they may not know how to say aloud.

The Christian witness must be truthful enough to challenge the mind and tender enough to understand the person.

That balance is difficult.

But it is necessary.

If Christianity is true, then it is not merely one private comfort among many. It is not a religious preference for those inclined toward spiritual things. It is not a decorative tradition to place around the edges of life.

It is a claim about reality.

About God.

About creation.

About human beings.

About sin.

About history.

About redemption.

About Jesus Christ.

That means the case for Christianity must not be afraid of evidence. It must also not misuse evidence as a weapon. Truth is not less true when spoken gently. And gentleness is not less gentle when it refuses to lie.

Why This Matters for Evangelism

Evangelism has often been reduced to technique.

Say this.
Ask that.
Quote this verse.
Avoid that objection.
Close the conversation.

There may be wisdom in learning how to speak clearly. But evangelism is not salesmanship. The goal is not to win a religious argument or pressure someone into agreement. The goal is faithful witness to truth.

That requires love.

It also requires clarity.

In our time, clarity must often begin much earlier than people expect. Before a person can understand sin, he may need to understand moral reality. Before he can understand salvation, he may need to understand that he is not an accident. Before he can understand Scripture, he may need to see that truth is not merely personal preference. Before he can understand Christ as Redeemer, he may need to recognize that the world itself bears marks of creation, meaning, and accountability.

That does not mean every evangelistic conversation must become a lecture on cosmology or DNA.

It means Christians should understand the world well enough to speak wisely when the questions come.

And the questions are coming.

They may sound scientific.

They may sound moral.

They may sound political.

They may sound personal.

They may come through grief, anger, sarcasm, curiosity, or quiet desperation.

But beneath many of them is the same deeper question:

What kind of world are we living in?

Veritology begins with this answer: We live in a world where truth exists, reason matters, evidence accumulates, and reality is more coherent under God than without Him.

That is not the whole Gospel.

But it may clear the ground so the Gospel can be heard.

The Pattern

Looking back, I can see that I was not discovering disconnected arguments.

I was discovering a pattern.

And somewhere along the way, the investigation had stopped feeling like something I was merely constructing. It had begun making demands on me as well. If I was going to ask other people to follow truth wherever it led, I would eventually have to be willing to do the same.

Again and again, the same movement appeared:

Evidence led to coherence.

Coherence led to meaning.

Meaning led toward Christ.

That is the path this book follows.

I am not asking the reader to pretend the questions are easy. They are not. Nor am I asking anyone to ignore suffering, doubt, abuse, hypocrisy, or the failures of religious people. Those things are real, and some will need to be faced directly.

But I am asking for something.

Let the evidence gather.

Do not lock the doors too early.

Do not keep science in one room, morality in another, history in another, and Jesus outside the house.

Open the doors.

Let truth speak across the whole of reality.

Then ask which worldview has the strength to hold it together.

That is the investigation.

That is Veritology.

And for me, it began with a simple realization I could no longer ignore:

Reality was not behaving like an accident.
