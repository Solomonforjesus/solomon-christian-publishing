Chapter Two

The Universe Had a Beginning

Steve was the first person who helped me understand that the universe was not merely large.

It was ordered.

That distinction matters.

Most people have looked up at the night sky and felt small. That is natural. The stars have a way of reducing our self-importance without saying a word. But Steve did not talk about the heavens mainly to make a person feel small. He talked about them to make a person think.

He had spent enough nights under open sky to know that wonder by itself was not the same as understanding. A meteor streaking across the dark might stir the imagination. A telescope might reveal beauty. A photograph might preserve a moment that disappeared in an instant.

But behind the beauty stood something deeper.

Law.

Structure.

Regularity.

The heavens were not random sparks thrown across a black ceiling. They moved according to a mathematical order that could be observed, measured, predicted, and studied.

That was where Steve’s mind lived.

He was not flashy about it. He did not turn every conversation into a lecture. But when he spoke about the universe, he had the patience of a man who knew the subject was too large for slogans. He had a way of letting the question breathe before answering it. I learned not to fill those silences.

The first few times we talked, I mistook them for hesitation. They were not. Steve was sorting the question before he answered it, separating what he knew from what he suspected and both from what he merely found interesting.

Once, after I had thrown three questions at him in less than a minute, he held up one hand.

“Walter, the universe has been here a while. Give me ten seconds.”

I laughed and did.

That became part of the rhythm between us.

And the first question was the largest one.

Did the universe have a beginning?

The Old Assumption

For much of intellectual history, the universe was often regarded as eternal.

The attraction of that view is understandable. If the universe had always existed, perhaps it required no beginning. And if it required no beginning, perhaps it required no Beginner. An eternal cosmos could function, at least philosophically, as a substitute for God.

The Expanding Universe

One of the great scientific shifts came when astronomers realized that, on the largest scales, galaxies were receding from one another. The universe was not static. It was expanding.

That matters because expansion points backward.

If galaxies are moving apart now, then in the past they were closer together. Follow that expansion backward, and the universe becomes denser and hotter.

This is the basic idea behind what most people call the Big Bang.

The phrase can mislead. It was not an explosion inside empty space, as though matter detonated in a dark room. Space itself was expanding. The early universe was unimaginably hot and dense, and what we see now is the result of that expansion over time.

For this investigation, the important point is not every technical detail of Big Bang cosmology.

The important point is simpler.

The universe appears to have had a beginning.

That does not prove every doctrine of Christianity. It does not settle every debate about Genesis. It does not tell us, by itself, the name of Jesus Christ.

But it does remove a comfortable hiding place.

If the universe began, then the universe is not the ultimate explanation of itself.

That is not a small conclusion.

Whatever begins to exist requires explanation, and a beginning raises the question of cause. And when the thing that begins is not a chair, a tree, or a star, but the entire physical universe, the question becomes unavoidable.

What caused the universe to exist?

Not what rearranged matter already there.

Not what happened inside the universe after it began.

What accounts for the existence of the universe itself?

That is where cosmology becomes more than a scientific subject. It becomes a doorway into metaphysics, theology, and meaning.

Steve helped me see that. I remember pushing back—not because I doubted that the universe had a history, but because I could feel how quickly the question moved from physics into something larger.

“So you’re saying cosmology gets us to God?”

Steve shook his head.

“No. I’m saying cosmology gets you to a question you don’t get to dismiss.”

That answer bothered me in the useful way.

He was not asking the science to preach a sermon. He was refusing to let me ask less of it than the evidence required.

He did not use the beginning of the universe as a cheap debating point. He treated it with care. The science deserved that. So did the question.

The Echo of the Beginning

The strongest evidence for a hot early universe did not come as a philosophical argument.

It came as a signal.

In 1964, Arno Penzias and Robert Wilson were working with a large horn antenna in New Jersey. They kept detecting a persistent background noise. It did not disappear when they adjusted the equipment. It did not vanish when they looked in different directions.

That stubborn signal turned out to be one of the most important discoveries in modern cosmology.

It was the Cosmic Microwave Background, often shortened to CMB.

Here is the idea in plain terms.

If the universe began in a hot, dense state, then light from that early period should still be present. But because the universe has expanded for billions of years, that ancient light would be stretched into microwave radiation. We would not see it with our eyes, but instruments could detect it.

That is what the CMB is.

It is not poetic language. It is measurable radiation coming from every direction in the sky. It is one of the strongest scientific witnesses that the universe was once hot, dense, and young.

Later missions mapped that radiation in remarkable detail. They found tiny temperature variations—small differences in the early universe that mattered enormously. Those fluctuations became the seeds from which large-scale structure developed. Without those fluctuations, matter would not have gathered into galaxies, stars, and eventually planets like Earth.

Without planets, no observers asking why anything exists.

That is the kind of detail that should slow a person down.

The universe did not merely begin.

It began in a way that allowed structure to form.

Too smooth, and matter never gathers.

Too chaotic, and stable structure never develops.

But the universe was neither uselessly smooth nor violently disordered. It had the kind of early variation that made later stars and galaxies possible.

Order did not merely appear.

Order persisted.

Entropy and the Wound-Up Clock

The Second Law of Thermodynamics is one of the most important ideas in physics.

Within an isolated system, energy tends to become more evenly distributed and less available for useful work. Entropy increases.

We see this everywhere.

A hot cup of coffee cools. A house left alone decays. A fire burns out. A machine wears down. Energy does not disappear, but it becomes less useful for doing work.

Now apply that idea to the universe.

If the universe had existed forever in its present physical condition, entropy would have had forever to increase. The stars would have burned out. Usable energy would have been exhausted. The universe would have reached heat death long before we arrived.

But we are here.

Stars still burn.

Energy still flows.

Life still exists.

That tells us something important. The universe has not been running forever. It had a beginning, or at least a finite past in the form we observe.

This also raises a second question.

Why did the universe begin in such an extraordinarily ordered state?

A universe beginning in thermodynamic equilibrium would not later generate the usable gradients required for stars, chemistry, and life.

But the early universe was not like that.

It began with remarkably low entropy. That means it began in a state of deep order, with the capacity for stars, galaxies, chemistry, planets, and eventually life.

This is not a small feature.

It is one of the foundational reasons there is a story to tell at all.

A universe that begins badly does not later rescue itself by wishing to become orderly. The beginning matters. Initial conditions matter. The starting state of the universe was not a throwaway detail. It was one of the first signs that reality had been given a structure capable of carrying life.

That was one of Steve’s strongest points.

The universe did not merely start.

It started well.

Fine-Tuning

Cosmology asks whether the universe had a beginning.

Fine-tuning asks why that universe is life-permitting.

The two questions are related, but they are not identical. A universe could have a beginning and still be sterile. It could expand too quickly or collapse too soon. It could lack stable atoms. It could fail to form stars. It could never produce carbon, oxygen, chemistry, planets, or living organisms.

A beginning gets us existence.

Fine-tuning asks why that existence has the right structure for life.

By fine-tuning, I mean this: certain features of the universe fall within narrow ranges that allow complex life to exist. If those features were significantly different, life would not merely be difficult. It would be impossible.

This includes things like the strength of gravity, the electromagnetic force, the strong nuclear force, the cosmological constant, and the conditions that allowed matter to gather into stars and galaxies.

The point is not that scientists have discovered one convenient number and Christians have overreacted.

The point is that life depends on a network of conditions. These conditions do not operate in isolation. Change one too much, and the system fails. Change another, and chemistry fails. Change another, and stars fail. Change another, and atoms fail.

Fine-tuning is not a claim that the universe is comfortable.

It is a claim that the universe is possible for life.

There is a difference.

The earth has storms, disease, deserts, earthquakes, and death. The universe is vast, dangerous, and mostly uninhabitable by us. But none of that removes the deeper fact that the underlying physical structure permits life at all.

That is the real question.

Why does the universe have the kind of structure that allows life to emerge?

Carbon and the Stars

One of the clearest examples involves carbon.

Carbon matters because life depends on it. Carbon atoms can form stable bonds in complex ways. They are central to organic chemistry. Without carbon, life as we know it does not exist.

But carbon is not produced casually.

Inside stars, carbon forms through what is called the triple-alpha process. In simple terms, three helium nuclei combine to form carbon. But that process only works efficiently because of a very specific energy level in the carbon nucleus.

Fred Hoyle recognized this before it was experimentally confirmed. He reasoned that because carbon exists in abundance, there had to be a particular resonance that allowed stars to produce it. If that resonance were not there, stars would not make enough carbon for life.

That resonance is not a vague convenience. It is a narrow nuclear condition. Inside stars, two helium nuclei first combine to form beryllium-8, which is extremely unstable. It exists only briefly. Briefly means almost unimaginably briefly. Beryllium-8 exists for only about 10⁻¹⁶ seconds before it falls apart. In that tiny window, a third helium nucleus must arrive and join the process. Carbon forms only because the Hoyle resonance, the excited state of carbon-12 at about 7.65 MeV, allows that reaction to proceed efficiently. This is not a generous margin. It is a nuclear doorway that must be open at the right energy, in the right sequence, for the right result.

That process depends on a specific excited energy state in carbon-12. If that energy level were slightly different, stars would produce far too little carbon for life. Shift the balance another way, and oxygen production is disrupted. The result is not merely less life, but the loss of the chemical foundation life requires.

Carbon is not produced in abundance simply because stars are powerful furnaces. It is produced because the nuclear structure of the universe permits a delicate sequence to succeed. The window is narrow. The timing is unforgiving. The outcome is essential.

That discovery should not be rushed past.

The carbon in our bodies was formed inside stars. But those stars could only make carbon in life-supporting abundance because nuclear physics operates within a narrow window. Slightly different values, and the process fails or produces the wrong balance of elements.

Too little carbon, and life cannot be built.

Too much instability, and the chemistry needed for life disappears.

The universe had to be the kind of universe where stars could produce the elements that living bodies require.

Hoyle was not a Christian apologist trying to force theology into physics. He was a brilliant scientist who followed the implications of his own work. His famous remark that a “super-intellect” had “monkeyed with physics” was blunt, memorable, and revealing.

He saw the pressure.

So should we.

The argument is not that carbon proves the entire Christian faith in one step.

It does not.

The argument is that carbon formation joins the larger pattern. The universe had a beginning, emerged from extraordinary order, and possesses the physical structure necessary for stars, elements, chemistry, and life.

That is not noise.

That is coherence.

The Problem with Easy Escape Routes

At this point, some people reach for the multiverse.

The idea is simple enough. Maybe there are countless universes with different laws and constants. If enough universes exist, then perhaps one of them would eventually have life-permitting conditions. We find ourselves in that one because we could not observe any other.

That may sound reasonable at first.

But it does not remove the deeper question.

If a multiverse exists, what explains it?

What gives it structure?

What governs its universe-generating process?

Why does it have the capacity to produce life-permitting universes?

Why is there something rather than nothing?

A multiverse may multiply possibilities, but multiplication is not explanation. Adding more universes does not automatically remove the need for a sufficient cause. It may simply move the question back one step.

This is where Veritology matters.

We are not trying to protect an answer from evidence. We are trying to follow truth wherever it leads. If the multiverse were demonstrated, we would have to think carefully about it. But an unobserved proposal should not be treated as stronger than the evidence it was invented to explain.

Fine-tuning still remains.

The universe we actually observe is life-permitting.

That fact demands interpretation.

What Steve Gave Me

Steve did not give me every answer.

He gave me a better starting point.

He helped me see that the universe is not a neutral backdrop to human life. It is part of the testimony. Its beginning, order, fine-tuning, and capacity to support life all matter.

None of that removes the need for Scripture.

None of it replaces Christ.

But it does challenge the claim that belief in a Creator is an intellectual embarrassment.

The greater intellectual difficulty may lie elsewhere: in standing before a universe with a finite past, a life-permitting structure, a low-entropy origin, mathematical order, and carbon-making stars, yet still treating accident as an explanation sufficient to account for it all.

Steve would have said that gently.

By then I had heard him often enough to know why. Precision did not need aggression. If the evidence was strong, it could bear the weight without being shouted.

I should remember that.

For me, cosmology opened the first major door.

The universe exists.

Its observable history is finite.

It emerged from an extraordinarily ordered early state.

It permits life.

It produces the elements life requires.

And the more carefully I looked, the harder it became to call that a cosmic accident.

The heavens were not the Gospel.

But they were not silent.

They pressed the first great question into my mind:

If reality begins with order, where did the order come from?

That question would lead me from the stars to mathematics.

Because the next surprise was not merely that the universe exists.

It was that the universe can be understood.
