Chapter Four

The World Was Made to Be Named

After Steve and Veronica, the investigation had gained a kind of order.

The investigation had already established a beginning, rational structure, and a universe open to understanding.

Those were not small claims. Each one carried weight. Cosmology had raised the question of cause. Mathematics had raised the question of intelligibility. Together, they pressed against the idea that reality was merely accidental.

But something was still missing.

A universe can be ordered without being personal. It can be measurable without being meaningful. Numbers can describe structure, but numbers do not tell us what grief is, or why a promise matters, or what a father means when he says he loves his child.

At some point, the investigation had to move from structure to meaning.

That is where Dawn entered the story.

Dawn loved words in a way most people do not.

Not casually. Not as decoration. Not as a hobby to fill quiet hours. Words had become part of how she survived the world and made sense of it.

There was a reason for that.

When she was young, an accident left a mark she could not simply set aside. I will not treat that story carelessly. Some injuries are not meant to become literary scenery. It is enough to say that the accident changed how she carried herself in public and taught her to protect certain places in her heart.

The scarf came later.

At first, I think it was partly shelter—a covering, a way to move through the world without answering every unspoken question in other people’s eyes. But over time, it became more than concealment. It became part of her presence, part of the way she faced the day.

Words served a similar purpose, but more deeply.

Dawn wrote constantly. Poems, fragments, letters, lines that sounded as though they had been pulled from somewhere painful and beautiful at the same time. She wrote with intensity, not because she wanted to sound poetic, but because ordinary speech often failed to carry what she meant.

That is what made her important to this investigation.

Dawn understood something about language before I knew how to define it.

Words do not merely label things.

They carry worlds. I once asked her why she cared so much about getting a word exactly right.

She thought about it longer than I expected.

“Because sometimes the wrong word leaves a person alone inside what happened to them.”

Then she looked down at the page in front of her.

“The right one can let somebody in.”

I did not have a better definition of why language mattered.

What Language Does

Language is easy to underestimate because we use it constantly.

We ask for coffee. We answer the phone. We tell children to be careful. We apologize. We joke. We pray. We argue. We read instructions, sign papers, send messages, and say goodnight.

Because language is ordinary, we forget how astonishing it is.

A sound leaves one mouth and becomes an idea in another mind.

Ink marks sit on a page and carry grief across centuries.

Pixels on a screen can deliver a command, a confession, a map, a lie, a poem, or a promise.

That should make us pause.

Language is not merely noise, marks, air pressure, ink, or light. Those are its physical carriers.

Meaning is the cargo.

That distinction matters.

The word “home” is not physically warm. The letters do not contain a kitchen, a porch, a family, or a memory. Yet the word can carry all of those things because a mind knows how to receive it.

Language depends on more than sound.

It depends on meaning.

And meaning depends on mind.

Symbols and Meaning

A symbol is something that points beyond itself.

The word “tree” is not a tree. It has no bark, roots, leaves, or rings. Yet when you read the word, your mind moves beyond the letters to the thing they signify.

That movement is so familiar that we barely notice it.

But it is one of the most important facts about human life.

A material mark can point to an immaterial meaning. A spoken word can refer to something absent. A sentence can describe the past, imagine the future, deny a falsehood, ask a question, or express love.

Matter can carry a message.

But matter is not enough to explain the message.

This is where crude materialism struggles. If all reality is only matter in motion, then meaning becomes difficult to account for. Not impossible to talk about, but difficult to ground. Chemistry can describe the ink. Physics can describe the sound wave. Neuroscience can describe brain activity while someone reads.

But none of those descriptions, by itself, explains aboutness.

Aboutness is the way words point beyond themselves.

The word “mercy” can refer to something no microscope can weigh. The word “justice” can name a moral reality no ruler can measure. The word “forgiveness” can carry a human act that changes a life without changing the mass of the room.

Language opens a window between minds.

That is not a small feature of reality.

The Structure Beneath Speech

Dawn’s love of words was emotional, but it was not careless.

She knew that language had structure.

Every human language has rules. Not always rules written in textbooks, but rules built into how speech works. Sounds are arranged in patterns. Words are formed from smaller meaningful pieces. Sentences are built with order. Meaning changes when structure changes.

“Dog bites man” does not mean the same thing as “man bites dog.”

The words are the same.

The order changes the meaning.

That tells us something important. Language is not just a pile of sounds. It is organized. It has levels. Sounds form words. Words form phrases. Phrases form sentences. Sentences carry thought.

And the system is astonishingly productive.

A child does not memorize every sentence he will ever say. He learns a structure that allows him to produce sentences he has never heard before. That is why a child can say something new, funny, mistaken, profound, or heartbreaking. He is not replaying recordings. He is building meaning.

This is one of the great differences between human language and animal communication.

Animals communicate. Some do it with remarkable sophistication. Birds sing. Bees dance. Whales call. Dogs signal. Primates warn and respond.

But human language is different in kind.

We do not merely signal danger or location. We discuss what might have happened, what should have happened, what never happened, and what we hope will happen. We tell stories. We make laws. We write poems. We confess sins. We ask whether the universe has a purpose.

A bee can point another bee toward nectar.

A man can ask why beauty hurts.

That difference matters.

The Human Fit for Language

Children learn language with astonishing speed.

They are not handed grammar manuals in the crib. They hear imperfect speech from imperfect people in noisy rooms, and yet they begin extracting structure. They learn words, patterns, meanings, and exceptions. They make mistakes, but their mistakes often show that their minds are searching for rules.

A child may say “goed” instead of “went.”

That is an error, but it is not a stupid error. It shows that the child has noticed a pattern: add a sound to mark the past. Walk becomes walked. Jump becomes jumped. So go becomes goed.

The child is wrong about that particular word, but right that language has structure.

This is one reason language is so difficult to reduce to mere sound imitation. Human beings are fitted for it. We are prepared to receive speech, produce speech, interpret speech, and use speech to share realities that are not physically present.

That word “fitted” is important.

A lock does not prove the existence of a particular key. But when a key fits a lock precisely, the correspondence calls for explanation.

Human minds are fitted for language, language is fitted to reality, and reality is stable enough to be named.

That gives us another piece of the larger pattern.

The world does not merely exist.

It can be understood.

And it can be spoken.

Dawn and the Weight of Words

This is why Dawn’s poetry mattered.

She had learned that words could do something appearances could not do. They could preserve what pain tried to scatter. They could name what embarrassment tried to hide. They could carry beauty out of injury.

A scarf could cover.

A poem could reveal.

That is not sentimental. It is true.

There are parts of human life that require language because silence cannot hold them alone. Grief needs words. Love needs words. Confession needs words. Worship needs words. Even when words fail, we know they have failed because we were reaching for meaning.

Dawn’s writing was not always polished, and it did not need to be. Its power came from urgency. She wrote as though meaning mattered because it did. She seemed to understand, almost instinctively, that a human being is not healed merely by having pain explained. Sometimes pain must be named.

That insight belongs in this book because evangelism depends on language.

The Gospel is not transmitted through mathematics or delivered by gravity. The heavens declare God’s glory, but they do not announce the death and resurrection of Jesus Christ as Scripture does.

That does not make language superior to the rest of creation. It makes language uniquely personal. The heavens declare glory, but words announce good news.

At some point, the Christian case must become verbal.

God speaks.

Prophets speak.

Apostles write.

Preachers proclaim.

The church confesses.

The sinner calls.

The saved worship.

Christianity is not merely a worldview to be inferred. It is also a message to be heard.

Aramaic and the Survival of Meaning

Dawn’s interest in language eventually moved toward Scripture, especially the way meaning survives across time, culture, and translation.

This matters because the Bible did not arrive as a private code in heaven’s own alphabet. It came through real human languages. Hebrew. Aramaic. Greek.

Aramaic is especially important because it served as a widely used language across successive Near Eastern empires. Portions of the Old Testament were written in Aramaic, most notably sections of Daniel and Ezra. That is not an accident of trivia. It reminds us that revelation entered history through actual languages spoken by actual people living under actual pressures.

Language carries meaning through change.

A word may shift form from one language to another. A sentence may need careful translation. A concept may wear different grammatical clothing. But meaning can survive the journey.

That is why translation is possible. No translation reproduces every feature of the source language perfectly. Translation requires knowledge, judgment, and care. But meaning can cross from one language to another because meaning is not identical to sound or grammatical form.

Consider the word “kingdom.”

The form may differ across languages. The sound may differ. The grammar may differ. But the concept can remain recognizable: rule, reign, dominion, authority, people under a king.

The form changes.

The meaning can remain.

That should matter to Christians. God chose to reveal truth through language, and He did so in a way that could cross borders, centuries, and cultures. The message did not remain trapped in one sound system. It could be translated, preached, read, copied, remembered, and carried.

Meaning has durability.

That durability is part of the miracle of language.

Logos

Eventually, Dawn’s love of words led me to John’s opening sentence.

“In the beginning was the Word.”

The Greek term is Logos.

Logos can carry a range of meanings, including word, speech, message, and reason. But John does not leave the term abstract. The Word is with God, the Word is God, and the Word later becomes flesh. John begins not with blind force, but with the eternal divine Person through whom all things were made.

Word.

Expression.

Meaning.

Person.

That is a staggering claim.

The Christian worldview does not treat language as a late accident in a meaningless universe. It places Word at the foundation of reality. Before human speech, before alphabets, before poetry, before translation, before Scripture was written by human hands, there was the eternal Word.

This does not mean every feature of language proves Christianity.

Again, we must not overstate the case.

But it does mean Christianity provides a coherent foundation for language and meaning that materialism struggles to supply. If reality begins with Mind, then meaning is not an intruder. If creation comes from the Word, then a speakable world is not surprising. If human beings are made in the image of God, then speaking creatures belong in a spoken creation.

Language is no longer a strange accident.

It is a family resemblance.

The World Was Made to Be Named

One of the earliest scenes in Genesis shows Adam naming the animals. That detail is easy to pass over. But naming is an act of recognition. It requires distinction. It requires attention. It requires the mind to notice what a thing is and then give that recognition verbal form.

That does not mean human beings control reality by naming it. We do not. A false name does not change the truth of a thing. But true naming is one of the ways human beings participate in understanding creation.

We name what we encounter.

We name animals, stars, diseases, elements, emotions, sins, virtues, children, places, and promises.

Sometimes we name badly.

Sometimes we name dishonestly.

Sometimes we use language to hide rather than reveal.

That is part of our fallenness. Words can bless, but they can also wound. A lie is possible only because truth exists. Deception borrows the machinery of meaning and turns it against reality.

That, too, is evidence of how powerful language is.

If words were merely sounds, lies would not matter.

But lies do matter.

So do promises.

So do vows.

So do prayers.

So does Scripture.

What Dawn Helped Me See

Dawn helped me see that language belonged within the larger pattern. It added something the earlier disciplines could not: the movement from intelligible structure to meaning itself.

Each step presses against the idea that human beings are accidental observers in a purposeless world. We are not merely looking at reality. We are able to speak truthfully about it. We can refer beyond ourselves. We can carry meaning from one person to another. We can hear testimony. We can receive revelation.

Dawn’s own life made that point personal.

She knew the difference between being seen and being understood. She knew that pain without language can become a prison. She knew that words, rightly used, could open a door. I began paying more attention after that—not only to whether my words were true, but to whether they actually carried what I meant. Dawn had made me see that precision was not merely intellectual. Sometimes it was an act of care.

That did not make her chapter soft.

It made it necessary.

Because the investigation was moving toward a deeper kind of information.

Language had shown me that symbols can carry meaning. It had shown me that meaning requires mind. It had shown me that communication is not reducible to the physical carrier.

Then Matthew would force the question into biology.

If human words can carry meaning through symbols, what should we make of a living cell using coded instructions?

The next evidence would not come from poetry.

It would come from DNA.
