Chapter Four

The World Was Made to Be Named

After Steve and Veronica, the investigation had gained a kind of order. It had established a beginning, rational structure, and a universe open to understanding.

Those were not small claims. Cosmology had raised the question of cause, and mathematics had raised the question of intelligibility. Together, they pressed against the idea that reality was merely accidental.

But something was still missing.

A universe can be ordered without being personal, measurable without being meaningful. Numbers can describe structure, but they do not tell us what grief is, why a promise matters, or what a father means when he says he loves his child.

At some point, the investigation had to move from structure to meaning. That is where Dawn entered the story.

Dawn loved words in a way most people do not. They were more than decoration or a hobby to fill quiet hours. They had become part of how she survived the world and made sense of it.

When she was young, an accident left a mark she could not simply set aside. I will not treat that story carelessly. Some injuries are not meant to become literary scenery. It is enough to say that the accident changed how she carried herself in public and taught her to protect certain places in her heart.

The scarf came later.

At first, I think it was partly shelter—a covering that let her move through the world without answering every unspoken question in other people’s eyes. Over time, it became more than concealment. It became part of her presence, part of the way she faced the day.

Words served a similar purpose, but more deeply.

Dawn wrote constantly: poems and fragments, letters and lines that sounded as though they had been pulled from somewhere painful and beautiful at the same time. The intensity came from what she needed to express. Ordinary speech often failed to carry what she meant.

She understood something about language before I knew how to define it. Words do not merely label things. They carry worlds.

I once asked her why she cared so much about getting a word exactly right.

She thought about it longer than I expected.

“Because sometimes the wrong word leaves a person alone inside what happened to them.”

Then she looked down at the page in front of her.

“The right one can let somebody in.”

I did not have a better definition of why language mattered.

What Language Does

Language is easy to underestimate because we use it constantly. We ask for coffee and answer the phone, tell children to be careful and read instructions. We use words to apologize, joke, pray, and argue. Signing papers, sending messages, and saying goodnight are so ordinary that we forget how astonishing language is.

A sound leaves one mouth and becomes an idea in another mind. Ink marks on a page carry grief across centuries. Pixels on a screen can deliver a command or a confession, show us a map, or carry a lie, a poem, or a promise.

The physical carriers are noise and marks, air pressure, ink, or light. Meaning is the cargo.

The word “home” is not physically warm. Its letters contain no kitchen or porch, no family or memory. Yet the word can carry all of those things because a mind knows how to receive it.

Language depends on meaning as well as sound, and meaning depends on mind.

Symbols and Meaning

A symbol is something that points beyond itself.

The word “tree” is not a tree. It has no bark or roots, no leaves or rings. Yet when you read it, your mind moves beyond the letters to the thing they signify. That movement is so familiar that we barely notice it, though it is one of the most important facts about human life.

A material mark can point to an immaterial meaning. A spoken word can refer to something absent. Through sentences, we describe the past and imagine the future. We can deny a falsehood, ask a question, or express love.

Matter can carry a message, but matter is not enough to explain it.

This is where crude materialism struggles. If all reality is only matter in motion, meaning becomes difficult to ground, even though we can talk about it. Chemistry can describe the ink, physics the sound wave, and neuroscience the brain activity of someone reading. None of those descriptions, by itself, explains aboutness: the way words point beyond themselves.

The word “mercy” can refer to something no microscope can weigh. “Justice” can name a moral reality no ruler can measure. “Forgiveness” can carry a human act that changes a life without changing the mass of the room.

Language opens a window between minds. That is not a small feature of reality.

The Structure Beneath Speech

Dawn’s love of words was emotional, but it was not careless. She knew that language had structure.

Every human language has rules, whether or not they are written in textbooks. Sounds follow patterns, words are formed from smaller meaningful pieces, and sentences are built with order. Change that structure, and the meaning changes.

“Dog bites man” does not mean the same thing as “man bites dog.”

The words are the same. Their order changes the meaning.

Language is organized at several levels. Sounds form words, which form phrases and sentences capable of carrying thought. This structure makes the system astonishingly productive.

A child does not memorize every sentence he will ever say. He learns a structure that allows him to produce sentences he has never heard before. He can say something new or funny, mistaken or profound, or something that breaks your heart. He is building meaning rather than replaying recordings.

This is one of the great differences between human language and animal communication.

Animals communicate, some with remarkable sophistication. Birds sing and bees dance. Whales call, dogs signal, and primates warn and respond. But human language is different in kind.

We can go beyond signaling danger or location to discuss what might have happened, what should have happened, and what never happened. We can speak of what we hope will happen. Our language allows us to tell stories and make laws, write poems and confess sins. We can ask whether the universe has a purpose.

A bee can point another bee toward nectar. A man can ask why beauty hurts.

That difference is important.

The Human Fit for Language

Children learn language with astonishing speed.

They are not handed grammar manuals in the crib. They hear imperfect speech from imperfect people in noisy rooms, yet they begin extracting structure. As they learn words and meanings, they recognize patterns and encounter exceptions. Even their mistakes often show that their minds are searching for rules.

A child may say “goed” instead of “went.”

That is an error, but it is not a stupid error. The child has noticed a pattern: add a sound to mark the past. Walk becomes walked, and jump becomes jumped, so go becomes goed. He is wrong about that particular word, but right that language has structure.

This is one reason language is so difficult to reduce to mere sound imitation. Human beings are fitted for it. We are prepared to receive and produce speech, interpret what others say, and share realities that are not physically present.

That word “fitted” is important.

A lock does not prove the existence of a particular key. But when a key fits a lock precisely, the correspondence calls for explanation.

Human minds are fitted for language, language is fitted to reality, and reality is stable enough to be named. That gives us another piece of the larger pattern. The world exists in a form that can be understood and spoken.

Dawn and the Weight of Words

Dawn had learned that words could do something appearances could not. Her poetry could preserve what pain tried to scatter, name what embarrassment tried to hide, and carry beauty out of injury.

A scarf could cover. A poem could reveal.

That is not sentimental. It is true.

There are parts of human life that require language because silence cannot hold them alone. We need words for grief and love, confession and worship. Even when words fail, we recognize the failure because we were reaching for meaning.

Dawn’s writing was not always polished, and it did not need to be. Its power came from urgency. She wrote as though meaning mattered because it did. She seemed to understand, almost instinctively, that a human being is not healed merely by having pain explained. Sometimes pain must be named.

That insight belongs in this book because evangelism depends on language.

The Gospel is not transmitted through mathematics or delivered by gravity. The heavens declare God’s glory, but they do not announce the death and resurrection of Jesus Christ as Scripture does.

That does not make language superior to the rest of creation. It makes language uniquely personal. The heavens declare glory, but words announce good news.

At some point, the Christian case must become verbal. God speaks, and prophets speak. Apostles write, and preachers proclaim. The church confesses, the sinner calls, and those who are saved worship.

Christianity is a worldview to be inferred and a message to be heard.

Aramaic and the Survival of Meaning

Dawn’s interest in language eventually moved toward Scripture, especially the way meaning survives across time, culture, and translation.

The Bible did not arrive as a private code in heaven’s own alphabet. It came through real human languages: Hebrew, Aramaic, and Greek.

Aramaic is especially important because it served as a widely used language across successive Near Eastern empires. Portions of the Old Testament were written in Aramaic, most notably sections of Daniel and Ezra. This reminds us that revelation entered history through languages spoken by people living under actual pressures.

Language carries meaning through change. Moving between languages may alter a word’s form or require a sentence to be carefully translated. A concept may wear different grammatical clothing, yet its meaning can survive the journey.

That is why translation is possible. No translation reproduces every feature of the source language perfectly. The work requires knowledge, judgment, and care. But meaning can cross from one language to another because it is not identical to sound or grammatical form.

Consider the word “kingdom.”

Its form, sound, and grammar may differ across languages, while the concept remains recognizable. It can convey rule or reign, dominion and authority, or a people under a king. The form changes; the meaning can remain.

That should matter to Christians. God chose to reveal truth through language in a way that could cross borders, centuries, and cultures. The message could be translated into another sound system, preached and read, copied and remembered, and carried to others.

That durability is part of the miracle of language.

Logos

Eventually, Dawn’s love of words led me to John’s opening sentence.

“In the beginning was the Word.”

The Greek term is Logos.

Logos can carry a range of meanings, including word, speech, message, and reason. But John does not leave the term abstract. The Word is with God, the Word is God, and the Word later becomes flesh. John begins not with blind force, but with the eternal divine Person through whom all things were made.

Word, expression, and meaning find their foundation in a Person. That is a staggering claim.

The Christian worldview places Word at the foundation of reality rather than treating language as a late accident in a meaningless universe. The eternal Word existed before human speech and alphabets, before poetry and translation, and before Scripture was written by human hands.

This does not mean every feature of language proves Christianity. We must not overstate the case. But Christianity provides a coherent foundation for language and meaning that materialism struggles to supply.

If reality begins with Mind, meaning is not an intruder. If creation comes from the Word, a speakable world is not surprising. If human beings are made in the image of God, speaking creatures belong in a spoken creation.

Language becomes a family resemblance rather than a strange accident.

The World Was Made to Be Named

One of the earliest scenes in Genesis shows Adam naming the animals. That detail is easy to pass over, but naming is an act of recognition. It requires attention to distinctions: the mind must notice what a thing is and give that recognition verbal form.

We do not control reality by naming it. A false name does not change the truth of a thing. True naming, however, is one of the ways human beings participate in understanding creation.

We name what we encounter. Animals and stars receive names, as do diseases and elements. We name emotions, sins, and virtues. We give names to children and places, and we name our promises.

Sometimes we do it badly or dishonestly, using language to hide rather than reveal. That is part of our fallenness. Words can bless, but they can also wound. A lie is possible only because truth exists. Deception borrows the machinery of meaning and turns it against reality.

That, too, is evidence of how powerful language is. If words were merely sounds, lies would not matter. Yet they do, just as promises and vows matter, and as prayers and Scripture matter.

What Dawn Helped Me See

Dawn helped me see that language belonged within the larger pattern. It added something the earlier disciplines could not: the movement from intelligible structure to meaning itself.

Each step presses against the idea that human beings are accidental observers in a purposeless world. We can speak truthfully about the reality we observe, refer beyond ourselves, and carry meaning from one person to another. Through language, we can hear testimony and receive revelation.

Dawn’s own life made that point personal.

She knew the difference between being seen and being understood. Pain without language could become a prison; words, rightly used, could open a door. I began paying more attention after that—not only to whether my words were true, but to whether they actually carried what I meant. Dawn had made me see that precision was not merely intellectual. Sometimes it was an act of care.

Her contribution was necessary to an investigation moving toward a deeper kind of information.

Language had shown me that symbols can carry meaning, that meaning requires mind, and that communication is not reducible to its physical carrier. Then Matthew would force the question into biology.

If human words can carry meaning through symbols, what should we make of a living cell using coded instructions?

The next evidence would come from DNA.
