What Does Instinct Know?

The Painted Lady, Quantum Biology, and the Hand of God in a Journey No Butterfly Completes

A multigenerational migration spanning Africa and Europe raises a deceptively simple question: what do we really mean when we call such behavior instinct?

By Gary Dearing · Solomon Christian Publishing · October 4, 2026

I was watching a nature program when the narrator described the migration of the Painted Lady butterfly, Vanessa cardui. I knew that butterflies migrated, of course, but one detail arrested my attention: this migration is so extensive that no individual butterfly completes the annual circuit. The journey is accomplished across successive generations.

That immediately changed the question for me.

How does the next butterfly participate in a journey that began before it existed?

The Painted Lady is nearly cosmopolitan, but the population moving between Africa and Europe has become particularly important to migration research. Studies now indicate an annual circuit extending roughly 12,000 to 15,000 kilometers between tropical Africa and northern Europe. The western migratory system can reach Scandinavia, and the complete cycle involves at least six generations. Stable-isotope studies have also demonstrated that individual Painted Ladies born in Europe can travel 4,000 kilometers or more into sub-Saharan Africa, crossing both the Mediterranean Sea and the Sahara.[1–3]

Those numbers are impressive, but distance alone is not what makes the migration intellectually compelling. A bird may undertake a migration remembered from previous journeys or travel within a social group containing experienced individuals. The Painted Lady presents a different problem. A butterfly that emerges somewhere along this intercontinental circuit has no personal memory of the route traveled by its parents. It has never seen the places from which they came. It receives no instruction from an older generation. Yet the population continues its seasonal movement, breeds, and produces another generation capable of continuing the larger cycle.

The familiar word for such behavior is instinct. That is a legitimate biological description, but it is not, by itself, a mechanism. In fact, the word becomes more interesting precisely when we refuse to let it end the inquiry.

What Does “Instinct” Actually Explain?

In biological usage, instinct generally refers to behavior with a substantial inherited component that does not require the kind of explicit learning associated with many human skills. That definition is useful, but it moves the investigation only one step forward. It tells us something about how the behavior is acquired; it does not tell us how the behavior is built.

The underlying problem is one of biological organization. Development must produce the sensory structures, neural circuitry, metabolic capacity, physiological timing, and behavioral responses required for migration. Those systems must then interact with information arriving from the environment. Photoperiod changes. Temperatures change. Vegetation appears and disappears with rainfall. Winds develop at particular altitudes and in particular directions. The position of the Sun changes not only from season to season but hour by hour. A migratory animal must somehow extract useful information from some combination of such variables and translate that information into behavior.

The Painted Lady does not require an internal map bearing the names Africa, Spain, France, or Scandinavia. What must be inherited is something biologically more plausible and, in its own way, more remarkable: a developmental and behavioral system capable of producing appropriate responses when particular internal and external conditions are encountered.

Research already shows that environmental conditions are deeply involved. Painted Lady population abundance in Europe is strongly linked to conditions in African breeding grounds, including rainfall and vegetation growth. Favorable high-altitude winds can facilitate enormous movements, and isotope evidence confirms repeated crossings of the Sahara in both directions. The migration is therefore neither a rigid march along a single invisible line nor a random dispersal. It is a dynamic biological system integrating inherited capacities with changing environmental information.[1–3]

This is where my original question—How does the next butterfly know?—requires refinement. The butterfly need not “know” in the conscious human sense. It does not need a mental representation of Africa or any conception of what its ancestors accomplished. Nevertheless, something objective persists across generations: the biological capacity to continue the population-level migration.

The parent dies; its memory dies with it. Yet hereditary information remains capable of constructing another organism that can enter the same larger system.

That is a much richer subject than the word instinct initially suggests.

And it is here that the Christian doctrine of creation becomes directly relevant. Scripture identifies the source of nature's existence and order without ambiguity:

“All things were made through Him, and without Him nothing was made that was made.”—John 1:3, NKJV

The biological mechanisms discovered by science are not rivals to God's creative action. They are mechanisms within the creation He made.

Navigation: What We Know and What We Do Not

Long-distance migration requires more than the urge to move. Direction matters.

For many animals, navigation involves several sensory systems rather than a single compass. Celestial information, internal circadian clocks, visual landmarks, wind, polarized light, olfactory cues, and the geomagnetic field can all contribute in different species and circumstances. Determining which signals a particular animal uses—and how its nervous system combines them—is one of the central challenges of movement biology.

This is where my earlier reading about the European robin returned to mind.

In Life on the Edge, physicist Jim Al-Khalili and molecular geneticist Johnjoe McFadden discuss one of the most intriguing ideas in modern biophysics: that some migratory animals may obtain directional information from Earth's magnetic field through chemical reactions whose behavior depends upon quantum mechanics.

The leading hypothesis involves molecules called cryptochromes. When an appropriate cryptochrome absorbs light, electron transfer can produce a transient pair of radicals—molecules containing unpaired electrons. The spins of those electrons can occupy different quantum states. Because the relative evolution of those spin states can be influenced by a magnetic field, the eventual chemical products of the reaction can, in principle, depend upon the orientation of the molecule relative to Earth's field.

This is known as the radical-pair mechanism.

The idea moved substantially beyond theory in 2021 when Jingjing Xu and colleagues reported in Nature that cryptochrome 4 from the European robin, Erithacus rubecula, exhibits magnetically sensitive photochemistry in vitro. The investigators identified a sequence of flavin–tryptophan radical pairs and showed that the robin protein was more magnetically sensitive under their experimental conditions than the corresponding proteins from chickens and pigeons.[4]

The distinction between what that experiment showed and what it did not show is important. It demonstrated magnetic sensitivity in robin CRY4 under laboratory conditions and provided a plausible molecular pathway for magnetoreception. It did not demonstrate every step by which a free-flying robin converts that molecular event into a navigational decision. The candidate mechanism is therefore serious, experimentally grounded science, but the entire sensory pathway is not yet settled.

That level of precision matters because my next question is obvious: could the Painted Lady employ something similar?

At present, we cannot responsibly say that it does. I am not aware of experimental evidence demonstrating a radical-pair compass in Vanessa cardui. But asking the question is scientifically reasonable because magnetosensation has been demonstrated in another migratory butterfly.

In 2021, Guijun Wan and colleagues showed that monarch butterflies respond to reversals of Earth's magnetic inclination under appropriate wavelengths of light. Using genetic manipulation, they found that the response depended upon the light-sensitive cryptochrome CRY1 and involved both the antennae and compound eyes.[5] Research in Drosophila has likewise produced evidence consistent with radical-pair magnetosensitivity, while also showing that the molecular mechanism may be more complicated than a simple model in which full-length cryptochrome alone acts as the magnetic receptor.[6]

These findings do not establish a quantum compass in the Painted Lady. They do establish something more modest and scientifically useful: insect magnetoreception can involve cryptochrome and radical-pair physics, making such a mechanism biologically plausible enough to investigate in other migratory insects.

The proper conclusion, then, is neither dismissal nor declaration. It is an open scientific question. The Painted Lady may use geomagnetic information; it may rely much more heavily on solar orientation, atmospheric conditions, or other cues; and several systems may operate together. Until the necessary experiments are performed, the mechanism should remain an hypothesis rather than a conclusion.

For me, that uncertainty does not diminish the subject. It makes it worth studying.

Science Describes Creation; It Does Not Create It

There is a persistent mistake in discussions of science and faith that becomes especially visible in subjects like this one. God and mechanism are sometimes placed on opposite sides of an explanatory contest: either God caused something, or physics did; either God designed life, or biology explains it; either God is responsible, or a molecular mechanism is responsible.

Those are not equivalent categories.

A scientific mechanism describes how events occur within the physical world. Christian theology addresses, among other things, why there is a physical world at all, why it possesses its particular order, and upon whom its continued existence depends. Discovering the workings of cryptochrome no more eliminates the Creator than discovering the workings of an eye eliminates the existence of light.

Indeed, the Christian claim is much stronger than a “God of the gaps” argument. God is not needed merely to occupy whatever territory science has not yet explained. Scripture declares Him sovereign over the territory we understand every bit as fully as over the territory we do not.

Suppose researchers eventually demonstrate, in exquisite molecular detail, how a Painted Lady determines direction. Suppose they identify every photoreceptor involved, trace the relevant neural pathways, characterize the genes affecting migratory state, model the atmospheric conditions that favor departure, and explain how environmental information changes reproductive physiology. Such an achievement would be magnificent science.

It would not have explained God away.

It would have described more completely something God made.

This is where I am comfortable making a statement that science itself cannot make, because it is a theological claim rather than an experimental measurement: God caused the science.

I do not mean that God substituted for the human investigator who designed an experiment or calculated a result. I mean that the entire enterprise of science occurs inside a reality that God brought into being. The matter being examined is His. The physical regularities being measured are His. The biological systems are His. The rational minds capable of studying them exist because He made human beings capable of rational inquiry.

Science depends upon an ordered creation before the first experiment can ever begin.

Scripture presents that creation in unmistakably Christ-centered terms. John writes that all things were made through the Son. Paul says of Christ:

“For by Him all things were created that are in heaven and that are on earth, visible and invisible... All things were created through Him and for Him. And He is before all things, and in Him all things consist.”—Colossians 1:16–17, NKJV

Those words reach from the largest scales of cosmology to the smallest structures examined by molecular biology. The sovereignty of Christ does not stop at the boundary of the cell, the genome, or the electron.

Scripture's account of creation is also Trinitarian. The opening verses of Genesis present God as Creator and the Spirit of God present over the waters; John's Gospel identifies the eternal Word, Christ, as the One through whom all things were made. Creation is not an independent machine that God once assembled and abandoned. It exists by His will, through His Son, under His sustaining power, and within His continuing sovereignty.

There is no scientific territory outside God's sovereignty. There is no molecular territory outside God's sovereignty. There is no quantum territory outside God's sovereignty.

That is not an argument against science. It is the theological ground upon which, science is possible.

Evolution, Mechanism, and the Question of Information

The Painted Lady's migration also touches evolutionary biology, and here precision matters again.

Natural selection is a genuine biological process. Heritable variation exists within populations, and traits affecting survival and reproductive success can change in frequency across generations. Migration itself is subject to evolutionary pressures. None of this should be denied merely because the biological system under study is astonishing.

At the same time, the term evolution can sometimes be asked to perform more explanatory work in popular discussion than the underlying science warrants. To say that a trait was shaped by selection is not yet to describe the molecular machinery producing that trait. Evolutionary history, developmental biology, sensory physiology, neuroscience, and behavioral ecology address related but distinct questions.

Natural selection has no foresight. It does not anticipate Africa, Scandinavia, the Sahara, next year's rainfall, or the position of the Sun. Selection describes differential persistence and reproduction among heritable variants within an already existing physical and biological world. The processes themselves operate within chemistry, heredity, ecological constraints, and the mathematical regularities of nature.

Therefore, evolutionary mechanisms do not sit above God as an alternative sovereign. They operate, insofar as they operate, within His creation.

The deeper scientific question in the Painted Lady remains wonderfully concrete: what hereditary and developmental architecture makes this migratory behavior possible?

DNA is obviously part of the answer, but saying “DNA” is no more complete an explanation than saying “instinct.” Genes function within regulatory networks; developmental processes build sensory organs and neural structures; those structures respond to the environment; physiological state alters behavior; and behavior changes the probability that an animal will reach favorable habitat and reproduce. The information involved is distributed through several levels of biological organization.

That observation connects directly with one of the central subjects of Fingerprint of Reality Science, Design and the Truth Beyond Matter: information is not peripheral to living systems. Biology depends upon the storage, transmission, regulation, interpretation, and use of information at every level.

The Painted Lady brings that fact into unusually sharp focus because the migration is larger than the life history of any individual participating in it. No butterfly needs to possess the complete route as a conscious plan. Yet the biological system is capable of producing a coherent continental pattern across generations.

For me, this is not a reason to stop with the word design and decline further investigation. It is the opposite. If creation is God's workmanship, then investigating the workmanship is an act of intellectual seriousness, not irreverence.

The Psalmist wrote:

“The works of the LORD are great,
Studied by all who have pleasure in them.”—Psalm 111:2, NKJV

Studied.

That word deserves emphasis. The proper response to an extraordinary natural phenomenon is not to protect it from explanation. Study it. Measure it. Test the hypothesis. Sequence the genes. Examine the proteins. Track the winds. Investigate the neurons. If a proposed quantum mechanism fails experimentally, discard it. If another mechanism survives repeated testing, follow the evidence where it leads.

Truth has nothing to fear from investigation because all truth belongs to God.

A Journey No Butterfly Completes

I began with a simple question: how does the next butterfly know that it is migrating, and where does it know to go?

The scientifically better question is now clearer. What inherited biological architecture, interacting with what environmental information, enables successive generations of Painted Ladies to participate in this enormous migratory system?

That question is answerable in principle, and portions of the answer are already emerging. We know that the migration is multigenerational. We know that individual butterflies can make extraordinary flights, including trans-Saharan journeys of thousands of kilometers. We know that climate, vegetation, wind, and season influence the population cycle. We know from other animals, including monarch butterflies and European robins, that magnetic information can be biologically useful and that cryptochrome-associated mechanisms deserve serious investigation. We do not yet know the complete sensory and molecular basis of Painted Lady navigation.

That is where the science presently stands.

But the scientific question is not the only question that can reasonably be asked.

Why is nature intelligible? Why does matter possess stable properties capable of supporting chemistry, heredity, sensation, and consciousness? Why can biological organisms acquire meaningful information from regularities in their environment? Why can the human mind, itself part of nature, uncover those regularities through mathematics and experiment?

Science is extraordinarily powerful at investigating mechanisms within that order. It does not establish that the order is self-existent, nor does methodological science require such a philosophical conclusion.

Scripture gives the deeper answer without hesitation.

The butterfly belongs to God. The Earth whose magnetic field may help guide living creatures belongs to God. The Sun used as a navigational reference belongs to God. The laws governing electrons belong to God. The DNA, the neuron, the wing, the atmosphere, and the human mind capable of examining all of them exist within His creation.

And above all, creation points beyond itself to Christ, through whom it was made and in whom it continues to exist.

The word instinct therefore remains useful to me, but it no longer sounds simple. Behind it lies developmental biology, sensory physiology, heredity, behavior, ecology, and perhaps, in some animals, quantum spin chemistry. Behind all of those mechanisms stands something deeper still: a created order whose coherence makes both life and science possible.

We began with a simple question about instinct, but the investigation led somewhere much richer. The Painted Lady’s migration involves heredity, environmental information, sensory biology, navigation, ecology, and perhaps mechanisms that reach into quantum physics. Science has already explained important parts of that system, and it will undoubtedly explain more.

None of that diminishes the Creator. It reveals more of the creation.

The butterfly does not need to comprehend the journey in order to participate in it. Science does not need to have every mechanism solved in order for the phenomenon to be real. And the Christian does not need gaps in scientific knowledge in order to see the hand of God.

The more clearly we understand the mechanism, the more clearly we see the workmanship.

And behind the mechanism, behind the information, behind the laws that make the mechanism possible, stands the One Scripture names without hesitation as Creator and Lord.

“For of Him and through Him and to Him are all things, to whom be glory forever. Amen.”—Romans 11:36, NKJV
